## Internship Basics

**Q1. What is the difference between an internship and a full-time job?**

An internship is a short-term, structured learning experience, typically 1 to 6 months, where you work at a company to gain industry exposure. A full-time job is a permanent or long-term employment contract with a fixed salary, benefits, and ongoing responsibilities.

Key differences:
- Internships pay a stipend, not a full salary
- Interns are expected to learn, not just deliver
- Internships have a defined end date
- Full-time roles come with PF, benefits, and legal employment protections

---

**Q2. How long should an internship be?**

Most internships in India run for 1 to 6 months. The ideal length depends on your goal:

- 1 to 2 months: Good for exploring a domain or adding a quick credential
- 3 months: The most common duration, enough to contribute meaningfully
- 6 months: Gives deeper exposure and significantly increases your chances of a PPO

Avoid internships shorter than 4 weeks unless it is for a specific project or competition. They rarely add meaningful weight to your resume.

---

**Q3. Can I do multiple internships at the same time?**

Technically yes, but it requires careful judgment.

It works if:
- Both are remote and part-time with low overlap in hours
- You can genuinely deliver quality work to both without cutting corners

It does not work if:
- Both require full-time commitment or on-site presence
- You end up doing mediocre work at both just to list two names

One internship done well is always more valuable than two done poorly.

---

**Q4. What is a PPO and how do I get one?**

A PPO (Pre-Placement Offer) is a full-time job offer extended to you by a company before your graduation or before campus placements begin, based on how well you performed during your internship.

How to increase your chances:
- Deliver consistent, high-quality work throughout the internship
- Go beyond assigned tasks when you see an opportunity to add value
- Build genuine relationships with your team and manager
- Communicate proactively and flag issues early rather than hiding them
- In the final weeks, express your interest in staying on and ask your manager about the possibility

Companies give PPOs to interns they feel are reliable and safe to hire, not just impressive on paper.

---

**Q5. What is the difference between off-campus and on-campus placements?**

- **On-campus placements:** Companies visit your college to recruit directly. You apply through your college placement cell and go through a structured hiring process on campus.
- **Off-campus placements:** You apply to companies independently through job portals, LinkedIn, cold emails, referrals, or direct applications on company websites.

On-campus placements depend heavily on your college's reputation and tie-ups. Off-campus is available to everyone and often offers more diverse opportunities.

## Stipend and Compensation

**Q6. Should I accept an unpaid internship?**

It depends on what you are getting in return.

Accept it if:
- The company is reputable and the name carries weight on a resume
- You are doing real work, not clerical tasks
- You have a mentor who is genuinely invested in your growth
- You can financially sustain yourself during this period
- There is a realistic path to a PPO or strong referral

Decline it if:
- Your work has no connection to your field
- There is no structure, guidance, or feedback
- The company is unknown and the experience will not open any doors
- The financial stress will affect your performance or well-being

> Employers never ask if you were paid. They ask what you built. But only if the experience was genuinely good.

---

**Q7. Can I negotiate my stipend as a fresher?**

Yes. It is acceptable and professional if done correctly.

When it makes sense to negotiate:
- You have a competing offer from another company
- Your skills or projects clearly add immediate value
- The company is a startup or mid-size firm with flexible budgets

How to do it:
- Research the average stipend for your role and city first
- Lead with your enthusiasm for the role, then bring up the number
- Base your ask on market data, not personal need
- Ask confidently but remain open to a counter

Large MNCs and structured programs often have fixed stipend bands with no room to negotiate.

---

**Q8. What documents should I get before joining?**

Before your first day, make sure you have received or confirmed:

- Offer letter with stipend amount, joining date, and duration clearly mentioned
- Mode of payment (bank transfer) and payment date
- Internship duration and work mode (remote or on-site)
- NDA or any agreement you are being asked to sign (read it carefully)
- Point of contact for onboarding

Get everything in writing. A verbal promise means nothing if a dispute arises later.

---

**Q9. What if the company delays or withholds my stipend?**

- Follow up politely via email, not just informally
- Address it to HR in writing so there is a paper trail
- If delays become a pattern, escalate to a senior or founder if it is a small startup
- If the company refuses to pay after repeated follow-ups, you have the right to leave and report the issue

For structured internships through Internshala or similar platforms, those platforms have dispute resolution processes you can use.

---

**Q10. Do I need to pay tax on my internship stipend?**

In India, it depends on the nature of the stipend:

- If your stipend is paid for services rendered (like a regular employee), it is treated as salary income and is taxable
- If it is a scholarship or educational grant, it is exempt under Section 10(16) of the Income Tax Act
- Most corporate internship stipends (engineering, CS, MBA) fall under the taxable category

Practically speaking:
- If your total annual income (including stipend) is below Rs. 2,50,000, you owe no tax
- If the company deducts TDS, they will issue a Form 16. You can file an ITR and claim a refund if your income is below the taxable limit
- Many companies do not deduct TDS on intern stipends, but that does not mean it is tax-free

> Keep a record of your stipend payments. File an ITR if your annual income crosses the basic exemption limit.

---

**Q11. What if the company pays late or irregularly?**

- Document all payment dates and amounts received
- Send a polite written follow-up to HR each time a payment is delayed
- If it happens more than twice, raise it formally before it becomes a bigger problem
- For startups, irregular payments can be a sign of cash flow issues. Factor this in when deciding whether to continue

---

**Q12. Should I ask for a stipend hike mid-internship if I am performing well?**

Yes, if you have been there for at least 3 to 4 weeks and have demonstrable results.

How to approach it:
- Wait for a feedback session or a one-on-one with your manager
- Mention specific contributions you have made and their impact
- Frame it professionally: "Given the work I have been able to contribute, is there room to revisit the stipend?"
- Be prepared for a no, and do not let it affect your work quality

Mid-internship hikes are rare but do happen, especially at startups that recognize performance quickly.

## Application and Job Search

**Q13. How early should I start applying?**

Earlier than you think.

- For summer internships: Start applying 2 to 3 months before your preferred joining date
- For off-campus year-round internships: Apply consistently throughout the year
- For campus placements: Stay connected with your placement cell from the start of your final year

Most students start too late and miss good opportunities. Companies fill positions on a rolling basis, so early applicants have a genuine advantage.

---

**Q14. How many companies should I apply to at once?**

There is no fixed number, but applying to only 2 to 3 companies is a mistake. Aim for at least 20 to 30 companies in the early phase and build from there.

The key is to maintain quality while applying consistently. Customize your resume for each role and actually read the job description before applying.

---

**Q15. Is Internshala reliable?**

Yes, for the most part. Internshala is one of the most widely used platforms for finding internships in India. Most listings are legitimate, and many freshers have secured good opportunities through it.

That said:
- Not every listing is high quality. Some unpaid roles offer little value
- Read the job description carefully and research the company before applying
- Be cautious of internships that require you to pay a fee or buy a course

Use Internshala as one of several platforms, not your only source.

---

**Q16. What if I get no replies after sending 50+ cold emails?**

This is common and does not mean your approach is failing. A 5 to 15% response rate is normal for cold outreach.

Check the following:
- Are your emails personalized or copy-pasted? Generic emails almost never get replies
- Is your subject line specific and relevant?
- Does your email clearly explain why you are reaching out to that company specifically?
- Is your resume and GitHub profile actually complete and presentable?

If all of those are in order, keep going. Increase your volume, try different people within the same companies, and refine your message based on what is not working.

---

**Q17. Is it okay to reach out to the same company again after 3 months?**

Yes. Companies change, teams grow, and hiring needs shift. If 3 months have passed since your last outreach with no response, it is completely fine to try again with a fresh message. Do not reference your previous email. Just write a new, updated one.

---

**Q18. What if a recruiter ghosts me after saying they are interested?**

This happens more often than it should. A few things to keep in mind:

- Follow up once after 7 to 10 days with a polite, brief message
- Follow up a second time after another week if there is still no response
- After two follow-ups with no reply, move on

Ghosting is unprofessional but very common. Do not take it personally and do not let it slow down your applications elsewhere.

## Resume and Profile

**Q19. How many projects should I have before applying?**

One well-built, functional, documented project is enough to start applying. Two strong projects make your profile significantly more competitive.

Quality matters far more than quantity. A single project you can explain confidently and defend technically is worth more than five half-built ones sitting on GitHub with no README.

---

**Q20. Should I list my CGPA on my resume?**

Generally yes, especially as a fresher, with these guidelines:

- If your CGPA is 7.0 or above on a 10-point scale, always include it
- If it is between 6.0 and 7.0, include it but complement it with strong projects and skills
- If it is below 6.0, you can omit it, but focus even more on practical work and certifications
- Always mention the scale clearly (e.g., 7.8 / 10) so recruiters can interpret it correctly
- Never round up or misrepresent your CGPA. Recruiters verify it during background checks

> Do not hide a low CGPA. Compensate for it with demonstrable skills.

---

**Q21. What coding platforms should I be active on?**

For CS students in India, these matter the most:

- **LeetCode:** Essential for DSA preparation and most SDE interviews
- **GitHub:** Where you show your actual code and projects
- **LinkedIn:** For professional visibility and networking
- **Codeforces or CodeChef:** Useful for competitive programming, valued at top companies

Pick one competitive platform and stay consistent. An active GitHub with real projects is often more impactful than a high contest rating on a platform no one at the company will check.

---

**Q22. Is LinkedIn really necessary as a student?**

Yes. LinkedIn is no longer just for experienced professionals.

As a student, it helps you:
- Get discovered by recruiters without applying anywhere
- Build connections with seniors and alumni who can refer you
- Find internship opportunities not listed on portals
- Establish your professional identity before graduation

Keep your profile updated with projects, skills, and internships. A complete LinkedIn profile with a clear headline regularly attracts inbound messages from recruiters.

## Interview and Selection

**Q23. What do companies actually check in a fresher interview?**

Most fresher interviews assess these things, roughly in order of importance:

- **Fundamentals:** DSA, OOPs, DBMS basics, OS concepts
- **Problem-solving ability:** Can you think through a problem logically?
- **Project understanding:** Can you explain what you built, why, and how?
- **Communication:** Can you articulate your thoughts clearly?
- **Attitude and learning ability:** Are you honest about what you do not know? Are you curious?

Companies are not expecting perfection. They are looking for someone who has a strong foundation, can learn fast, and will not be a liability on the team.

---

**Q24. How many interview rounds does a typical internship have?**

It varies by company size and role:

- Small startup: 1 to 2 rounds (often just a technical call and a culture fit chat)
- Mid-size company: 2 to 3 rounds (aptitude or coding test, technical, HR)
- Large MNC or product company: 3 to 5 rounds (online assessment, DSA rounds, system design basics, HR)

Some companies also include a take-home assignment or a short project submission before the technical rounds.

---

**Q25. What should I do if I get rejected?**

- Do not take it personally. Rejection is a normal part of the process
- If possible, ask for feedback. Not every recruiter will respond, but some do
- Identify what you could have prepared better and work on it
- Keep your applications active. Do not pause and wait for one company's result
- Every rejection brings you closer to understanding what the process actually requires

Most people who land good internships went through many rejections first.

## Remote vs On-Site

**Q26. Which is better for a fresher, remote or on-site?**

For most freshers, on-site or hybrid is the better option, at least in the first 1 to 2 years.

In-person work gives you:
- Direct access to seniors for quick questions and guidance
- Faster learning through observation and proximity
- Natural networking that does not require extra effort
- Visibility to your manager and team

Remote works as a fresher if the company has strong mentorship systems and you are highly self-directed. But if you are still building your fundamentals, on-site dramatically accelerates that process.

---

**Q27. How do I stay productive in a remote internship?**

- Set a fixed start and end time every day and stick to it
- Communicate your progress proactively; do not wait to be asked
- Over-communicate when in doubt. It is better to send one extra message than to go silent for a day
- Keep your camera on during meetings unless told otherwise
- Block distractions during work hours and treat your workspace as an office
- Reach out to your mentor or teammates regularly, not just when you are stuck

The biggest risk in remote internships is becoming invisible. Make sure your work and your presence are consistently felt.

## Mindset and Reality

**Q28. Is it okay to feel lost or unproductive in the first few weeks?**

Completely normal. Almost everyone feels this way, regardless of how prepared they were.

The first few weeks are for orientation, understanding the codebase or workflow, setting up your environment, and figuring out how the team operates. Very little actual output is expected in week one or two.

Give yourself the grace period. Ask questions without hesitation. The discomfort of feeling lost is exactly where learning happens.

---

**Q29. What if my internship work feels too easy or boring?**

Talk to your mentor. Let them know you are comfortable with the current tasks and want to take on more.

Most managers appreciate interns who ask for more responsibility rather than sitting idle. You can say something like: "I have finished the tasks assigned to me. Is there something more challenging I can pick up?"

If after several weeks the work is still well below your ability and the company cannot offer anything more meaningful, it may be worth reassessing whether this internship is the right one for you.

---

**Q30. What if I am the only intern and have no peer to compare with?**

This is actually more common than people think, especially at startups.

Focus on building relationships with your team instead. Your colleagues, even if senior, can become your informal mentors. Ask them about their experience, how they approach problems, and what they wish they had known earlier.

You do not need another intern to benchmark yourself. Compare your progress against your own week-one self.

---

**Q31. Should I pretend to know something I do not during work?**

No. It always backfires.

Saying "I am not sure about this, but I can look into it and get back to you" is not a weakness. It is what experienced professionals actually do.

Pretending to know something and then delivering wrong or broken work wastes everyone's time, including yours, and damages the trust your team has in you. Honesty about your gaps, combined with genuine effort to fill them, is always the better move.

## Practical Situations

**Q32. What if the company changes my role after joining?**

This happens, especially at startups.

- First, understand why. Sometimes it is a genuine business need, sometimes it is disorganization
- If the new role is still within your domain and offers learning, give it a fair shot
- If the new role is completely unrelated to what you signed up for and you are getting no value, raise it with HR or your manager directly
- If there is no resolution, it is acceptable to leave professionally and look for something better

You agreed to a specific role. A significant change without discussion is something you have every right to address.

---

**Q33. What if my mentor is unavailable or unhelpful?**

- Start by giving it time. Mentors are often busy and may not be available on demand
- Schedule a fixed weekly check-in rather than expecting ad hoc availability
- If your assigned mentor is genuinely disengaged, identify another senior on your team who seems approachable and build that relationship organically
- Use async communication. Send a clear, specific question over chat rather than waiting for a live call

If you are completely without any guidance and the internship is not progressing, raise it with HR.

---

**Q34. Can I leave an internship midway if it is not working out?**

Yes, you can. An internship is not a permanent legal commitment.

If you decide to leave:
- Give reasonable notice, at least 1 to 2 weeks if possible
- Inform your manager professionally and thank them for the opportunity
- Do not burn bridges, even if the experience was poor
- Make sure you collect whatever certificate or proof of work you are entitled to

Leaving an internship early is not ideal for your resume, but staying in something genuinely harmful or useless is worse.

---

**Q35. What if the company asks me to work beyond agreed hours?**

- Occasional extra hours during a deadline or critical phase are normal and part of any professional environment
- If extra hours become a consistent expectation without acknowledgment, raise it calmly with your manager
- For interns, working beyond the agreed scope should come with either a formal extension of the role or at least recognition
- If it crosses into exploitation with no learning or compensation, you have the right to set boundaries or leave

---

**Q36. What if I made a mistake that affected the team or project?**

Own it immediately. Do not hide it.

- Tell your manager or team lead as soon as you realize it
- Come with a clear explanation of what happened and, if possible, a proposed fix
- Offer to work on correcting it

Most managers respect interns who take accountability far more than those who stay silent or deflect. A mistake followed by honest ownership and a solution almost never ends badly.

## During and After Internship

**Q37. What should I do on my first day?**

- Set up your development environment and accounts properly
- Introduce yourself to your team, even via a quick message in the group chat
- Ask for a clear overview of your role, the project you will be working on, and the tools the team uses
- Note down any access credentials or setup instructions you receive
- Do not try to impress anyone on day one. Listen, observe, and ask smart questions

First-day anxiety is normal. Focus on understanding the environment rather than proving yourself.

---

**Q38. How do I ask for a Letter of Recommendation?**

- Ask at least 1 to 2 weeks before your last day, not on the final day
- Ask the person who worked most closely with you and can speak specifically to your contributions
- Send a brief message: "I really valued the experience working with you. Would you be comfortable writing a letter of recommendation for me?"
- Optionally share your resume and a few highlights from your work to make it easier for them
- Always follow up with a thank-you once you receive it

---

**Q39. Should I connect with my colleagues on LinkedIn?**

Yes, always. Your internship network is one of the most valuable things you take away.

- Send connection requests to your team members, manager, and anyone you worked with closely
- Add a personal note when connecting: "It was great working with you at [Company]. Looking forward to staying in touch."
- Engage with their posts occasionally so the connection stays warm
- These people can refer you to future roles, provide recommendations, or become collaborators years later

---

**Q40. What should I do if the internship is not going well?**

First, identify why.

- Is it the work itself, the environment, your own performance, or a mismatch in expectations?
- Have an honest conversation with your manager. Ask for feedback and clarity on what is expected
- Give yourself 2 to 3 weeks to course-correct before making any decision to leave
- If the issue is a toxic environment, lack of any learning, or role misrepresentation, it is okay to leave after handling it professionally

Not every internship will be great. What matters is that you extract whatever learning you can and move forward.

## Documentation and Proof

**Q41. What if the company refuses to give an internship certificate?**

- Follow up in writing, specifically via email, so there is a record
- Reference your offer letter which typically implies a certificate on completion
- If they continue to refuse, you can use your offer letter, emails, and bank payment records as proof that the internship happened
- For disputes, platforms like Internshala have escalation mechanisms

Always ask for your certificate at least a week before your last day, not after.

---

**Q42. Is a certificate from a small or unknown company still valuable?**

Yes, if the work you did is real and you can speak to it.

A certificate from an unknown startup where you built something meaningful is worth more than a certificate from a well-known company where you did clerical work. The work and the skills matter more than the company name, especially in technical interviews where you will be asked to explain what you actually did.

---

**Q43. Should I keep copies of my work before leaving?**

Yes, within appropriate boundaries.

- Save code you wrote to a private GitHub repository if the company permits it
- Keep copies of documents, presentations, or reports you created, as long as they are not confidential
- Do not share or publish proprietary code or client data
- Having evidence of your work helps you explain your experience clearly in future interviews

Read any NDA you signed to understand what you can and cannot retain.

---

**Q44. What counts as proof of internship if I worked informally?**

If you worked without a formal offer letter, gather whatever you have:

- Email threads with your manager or team
- Bank statements showing stipend payments
- WhatsApp or Slack messages with task assignments
- Any work output, code, documents, or designs you contributed to
- A LinkedIn endorsement or recommendation from someone at the company

Informal internships are common in India, particularly at small startups. What you built and what you learned still has value even without a formal certificate.

## Long-term Thinking

**Q45. Does a bad internship hurt my resume?**

Not necessarily. What matters is how you talk about it.

If you learned something, however small, you can frame it positively in an interview. If the internship was genuinely unproductive, you do not need to give it much weight on your resume. Focus instead on the projects, skills, or observations you took away from it.

A bad internship only hurts you if it is the only thing on your resume and you have nothing else to show. That is a reason to build more.

---

**Q46. How do I explain a gap between internships?**

Be honest and frame it around what you did during that time.

If you used the gap to learn, build projects, complete certifications, or prepare for competitive exams, say that directly. Recruiters respect self-directed learning.

If the gap was for personal reasons like health, family, or financial circumstances, a brief, matter-of-fact explanation is enough. You do not owe anyone a detailed account.

> Never lie about or try to hide a gap. A short honest explanation is always better than a story that does not hold up.

---

**Q47. Should I mention a very short internship (less than a month) on my resume?**

It depends on what you did.

- If you contributed to something real and can speak to it technically, include it
- If the internship ended early because of circumstances outside your control, a brief mention is fine
- If it was less than 2 weeks and you did nothing of substance, leave it off. It raises more questions than it answers

When in doubt, ask yourself: "If an interviewer asks me about this, will I have something meaningful to say?" If yes, include it. If not, skip it.

---

**Q48. How many internships is too many before getting a full-time job?**

There is no hard ceiling, but there is a point where it starts raising questions.

If you have 3 or more internships and no full-time role after graduation, some recruiters will wonder why. The concern is usually: "Is this person avoiding commitment, or are they not getting offers?"

To avoid this perception:
- Make sure each internship shows progression in responsibility or domain
- At some point, transition from intern mode to actively pursuing full-time roles
- If you are doing internships to fill a gap while job searching, that is completely valid. Just be ready to articulate your timeline clearly

Two to three well-chosen internships before your first full-time role is a strong position to be in.

---

## Final Note

No FAQ can cover every situation. The internship experience is different for everyone and changes with every company, team, and individual.

What stays consistent is this:

- Show up with genuine curiosity
- Communicate honestly and proactively
- Do not measure everything against what someone else is getting
- Every experience, even a bad one, teaches you something if you pay attention

> The best preparation for your next opportunity is doing your current one well.
